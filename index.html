Food and fuelling guide

Every day

  • Build most meals from a carb, a protein, and vegetables or fruit.
  • Aim for about 1.4–1.7 g of protein per kg of body weight, spread across the day.
  • Eat more carbs on and before bigger training days. Don't diet hard during the plan: under-eating raises injury risk.

Before a run

  • Under 60 minutes: a normal meal earlier, or a small snack if you're hungry. Many people run fine on nothing for short easy runs.
  • Long runs: a carb-rich meal 2–3 hours before, such as a bagel with peanut butter and a banana, or oatmeal with honey.
  • Closer to the start, keep it small: half a banana or a slice of toast.

During a run

  • Runs over about 75 minutes: take 30–60 g of carbs per hour. One gel is usually 20–25 g, so read the label.
  • Start around minute 45 and repeat every 30–40 minutes, with water.
  • Gels, chews and sports drink all work. Find what your stomach likes, because that's what you'll use on race day.

After a run

  • Within about an hour of harder runs, have carbs plus 20–30 g of protein.
  • Easy options: chocolate milk, Greek yoghurt with granola, eggs on toast, or a rice bowl with chicken.
  • Rehydrate until your urine is pale yellow.

Hydration

  • Drink to thirst through the day, and more in heated rooms or on the Peloton.
  • On runs over an hour, carry fluid or plan a route past water.
  • Add electrolytes on long or sweaty sessions. Don't force down large amounts of plain water.

Race days: 2–4 April

  • Friday and Saturday: make carbs the biggest part of each meal, and cut back on high-fibre and very fatty foods.
  • Sunday: eat your rehearsed breakfast 2–3 hours before the start, sip water, and carry your rehearsed fuel.
  • The rule: nothing new on race day. That covers food, gels, shoes and kit.

This is general guidance, not medical or dietetic advice. Talk to your doctor before starting if you have a health condition, and see them or a sports dietitian about supplements, ongoing fatigue, or a history of disordered eating.

How to use the plan

How hard is easy?

You can speak in full sentences. Most of your running should feel like this, even if it means slowing down as the distances grow.

Why the Bike and the road split

Your legs need to get used to running, so from week 9 the hard running happens outdoors on Tuesdays. The Bike builds fitness without the impact. Your week 2 FTP test sets your Power Zones, and Zones 2–3 should feel steady enough to talk in short sentences.

Bad weather and dark evenings

Ice, lightning or dangerous cold? Ride the Bike for the time your run would take, using Zone 4 for any hard efforts. Try to move a long run to another day rather than replacing it. On dark evenings, stick to well-lit routes and wear something reflective.

Missed a session?

Let it go and carry on with today's session. Never double up. If you miss a full week, repeat the last week you completed. Protect the long run first.

Pain

Muscle soreness is normal. Sharp pain, pain that changes your stride, or pain that gets worse as you run means stop and rest for two days. See a doctor or physio if it's still there.

Saturday parkrun

Run it easy, because the long run comes the next morning. Weeks 4, 8 and 12 are the exceptions: Sunday is short, so race it and track your time. If your parkrun is cancelled, run 5 km on your own or ride for 30 minutes.

What is race effort?

Harder than your long runs but still controlled, at a pace you could hold for about two hours. Your parkrun times and the week 16 tune-up will help you find it.

Shoes

Get running shoes fitted now, and a fresh pair by late February so they're worn in by race day.
